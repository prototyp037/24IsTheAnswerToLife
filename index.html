Eigenvector Word Embeddings

How a word's position in an eigenvector matrix becomes its vector representation.

The question

Which vector represents word \(e_i\)?

If \(V=[v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_c]\) is the eigenvector matrix of a co-occurrence matrix \(C\), what is the eigenvector embedding of word \(e_i\)?

The answer

Take row \(i\) of \(V\).

In row-vector form, the embedding is:

\[ \boxed{\operatorname{embedding}(e_i) =e_i^\top V =\begin{bmatrix}(v_1)_i & (v_2)_i & \cdots & (v_c)_i\end{bmatrix}} \]

If you prefer a column-vector embedding, transpose the row:

\[ \boxed{V^\top e_i =\begin{bmatrix}(v_1)_i\\(v_2)_i\\\vdots\\(v_c)_i\end{bmatrix}} \]
Remember: The columns of \(V\) are eigenvectors; the rows of \(V\) give word embeddings.

The intuition

Why does this work?

Each eigenvector defines a direction in the co-occurrence space. Word \(i\) has one coordinate along each direction, and those coordinates form its embedding.

1

Construct \(V\)

Place the chosen eigenvectors \(v_1,\ldots,v_c\) in the columns of \(V\).

2

Select word \(i\)

Use the one-hot basis vector \(e_i\), whose only nonzero entry is at index \(i\).

3

Read its coordinates

Multiply \(e_i^\top V\) to extract exactly the \(i\)-th row.

\[ (e_i^\top V)_j=e_i^\top v_j=(v_j)_i \]

Worked example

A two-word vocabulary

Consider the symmetric co-occurrence matrix \(C\) and an orthonormal eigenvector matrix \(V\):

\[ C=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{bmatrix}, \qquad V=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \begin{bmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{bmatrix} \]

Word \(e_1\)

Take the first row:

\[ e_1^\top V=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix}1&1\end{bmatrix} \]

Word \(e_2\)

Take the second row:

\[ e_2^\top V=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix}1&-1\end{bmatrix} \]

A useful detail

What if we keep only \(k\) eigenvectors?

For a vocabulary of \(n\) words, let \(V_k\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}\) contain \(k\) selected eigenvectors. Then each word receives a \(k\)-dimensional embedding:

\[ \operatorname{embedding}_k(e_i)=e_i^\top V_k\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times k} \]

This is specifically the eigenvector-only embedding. Some spectral embedding methods also weight coordinates by eigenvalues; that is a different convention.
