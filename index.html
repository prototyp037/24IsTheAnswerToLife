Question 1 · Second derivative test

Where can the absolute minimum occur?

Consider \(f(x,y,z)\) on the closed ellipsoid

\[\displaystyle x^2+\frac{y^2}{4}+\frac{z^2}{9}\le 1.\]

The only interior critical points are \(a\), \(b\), and \(c\), with Hessians:

\[ D^2f(a)=\begin{bmatrix}3&0&0\\0&-2&1\\0&1&-1\end{bmatrix}, \quad D^2f(b)=\begin{bmatrix}2&0&0\\0&2&0\\0&0&2\end{bmatrix} \]
\[ D^2f(c)=\begin{bmatrix}-2&1&0\\1&1&0\\0&0&1\end{bmatrix} \]

Correct choice · 5

The minimum value of \(f\) could occur at \(b\).

Reasoning

  1. \(D^2 f(b)=2I\) is positive definite, so \(b\) is a strict local minimum.
  2. \(D^2 f(a)\) has both positive and negative curvature (e.g. diagonal entries \(3\) and \(-2\)); \(a\) is a saddle, not a minimum or maximum.
  3. \(D^2 f(c)\) also has both signs (\(-2\) and \(1\)); \(c\) is a saddle.
  4. The ellipsoid is closed and bounded. Since \(f\) is differentiable, it is continuous and attains both an absolute maximum and an absolute minimum somewhere in the region.

The Hessian test tells us what happens locally. It does not guarantee that \(b\) is the absolute minimum; the boundary may contain a smaller value. Hence could, not must.

Question 2 · Reversing integration order

Evaluate the double integral

\[ I=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{x^2}^{1}\frac{\sqrt y}{1+y^2}\,dy\,dx \]

Exact answer

\(\displaystyle \boxed{\frac{\ln 2}{2}}\)

Step 1 · Describe the region

The original bounds say \(0\le x\le1\) and \(x^2\le y\le1\). Switching the order gives:

\[ 0\le y\le1,\qquad 0\le x\le\sqrt y. \]

Step 2 · Integrate with respect to \(x\)

\[ \begin{aligned} I&=\int_0^1\int_0^{\sqrt y}\frac{\sqrt y}{1+y^2}\,dx\,dy\\[3pt] &=\int_0^1\frac{\sqrt y}{1+y^2}(\sqrt y)\,dy\\[3pt] &=\int_0^1\frac{y}{1+y^2}\,dy. \end{aligned} \]

Step 3 · Integrate using substitution

\[ \begin{aligned} I&=\left[\tfrac12\ln(1+y^2)\right]_0^1\\ &=\boxed{\tfrac12\ln 2}. \end{aligned} \]

Fast recognition: The integrand contains \(\sqrt y\) and the reversed \(x\)-width is \(\sqrt y\), so their product simplifies to \(y\).

Question 3 · Constrained optimization

Find the closest point to the origin

Find the point or points on this surface closest to \((0,0,0)\):

\[ 2x^2+y^2-(z-1)^2=1 \]

Two equally close points

\(\displaystyle \boxed{\left(\pm\frac{\sqrt{26}}{6},\,0,\,\frac13\right)}\)

Minimum distance: \(\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac56}\)

Step 1 · Minimize squared distance

It is easier to minimize \(D^2=x^2+y^2+z^2\) than its square root. Rearrange the constraint:

\[ 2x^2+y^2=1+(z-1)^2. \]

Step 2 · Use the constraint

For fixed \(z\), we have

\[ x^2+y^2=\frac{1+(z-1)^2}{2}+\frac{y^2}{2}. \]

The right side is smallest when \(y=0\). Thus

\[ D^2=\tfrac12\big(1+(z-1)^2\big)+z^2 =\tfrac32z^2-z+1. \]

Step 3 · Minimize over \(z\)

\[ \frac{dD^2}{dz}=3z-1=0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad z=\frac13. \]

Now substitute \(y=0\) and \(z=\frac13\) into the constraint:

\[ 2x^2=1+\left(-\frac23\right)^2=\frac{13}{9} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x=\pm\frac{\sqrt{26}}6. \]

Finally, \(\displaystyle D^2=\frac56\), so \(\displaystyle D=\sqrt{\frac56}\).

This method establishes the global minimum directly, without needing to compare multiple Lagrange-multiplier candidates.

Previous question · AI / Linear algebra

Eigenvector word embedding

If \(V=[v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_c]\) is the eigenvector matrix of a word co-occurrence matrix \(C\), what is the eigenvector embedding of word \(e_i\)?

Answer

Take the \(i\)-th row of \(V\).
\[ \operatorname{embed}(e_i) =e_i^\top V =\begin{bmatrix}(v_1)_i&(v_2)_i&\cdots&(v_c)_i\end{bmatrix}. \]

As a column vector, the same embedding is

\[ V^\top e_i= \begin{bmatrix}(v_1)_i\\(v_2)_i\\\vdots\\(v_c)_i\end{bmatrix}. \]

Why?

\(e_i\) is the one-hot basis vector for word \(i\). Multiplying \(e_i^\top V\) selects the \(i\)-th row, which contains that word's coordinates in each eigenvector direction.

Columns of \(V\) are eigenvectors; rows of \(V\) are word embeddings under the eigenvector-only convention.
